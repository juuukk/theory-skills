# Phenomenal Design — Presence, Experience & Non-Ordinary States

This reference file covers frameworks that describe the structure of lived experience at a depth that functional design theory doesn't reach: how embodied presence works, what peak and non-ordinary states are, why threshold spaces alter people, and what the sublime means as a design target. Where relevant, contested or speculative frameworks are labeled as such.

---

## Heidegger — Ready-to-Hand / Present-at-Hand

Martin Heidegger's distinction from *Being and Time* (1927) is the philosophical grounding for what Weiser called "calm technology" — arrived at from the opposite direction.

**Ready-to-hand (Zuhandenheit):** A tool in use disappears from conscious awareness. The hammer is not an object the carpenter perceives — it is an extension of intention, transparent to the task. Skilled tool use is characterized by this transparency. The expert typist doesn't perceive the keyboard; they perceive the text.

**Present-at-hand (Vorhandenheit):** When the tool breaks, is unfamiliar, or fails to work as expected, it suddenly becomes an object of conscious attention. The hammer is noticed as a hammer. The interface is noticed as an interface. The spell of readiness-to-hand is broken.

**Design implication:** the ideal state for an interface is ready-to-hand — so fluent, so well-matched to the user's intention, that it disappears. Every moment of "noticing the interface" is a moment of present-at-hand breakdown.

Design that is noticed is design that has partially failed — unless the design's purpose is to be noticed (art, spectacle). Functional design aims for invisibility through quality. An aesthetic-forward interface (a dark HUD, ornate visual chrome, high-style UI) is an interesting case: the aesthetic *is* the point (the visual style is desired), but the *function* should still be ready-to-hand within that aesthetic. The tool should be beautiful and invisible in use.

## Merleau-Ponty — Embodied Cognition

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) argued that consciousness is not a brain in a body but a body in a world. The body is not the instrument of thought — it is the ground of all experience.

**The body schema:** a pre-reflective awareness of the body's position, capacities, and extension into the environment. The skilled musician's fingers know where the notes are. The dancer's body knows the floor before conscious calculation. This knowledge is bodily, not propositional.

**Tool incorporation:** through use, tools become part of the body schema. The blind person's cane is not perceived as an object touching the ground — the cane's tip becomes a sensory surface of the body. Skilled drivers don't feel the car as a separate object; they feel the road through the tyres.

**For interface design:** users who have deeply incorporated a gesture, a shortcut, a swipe pattern experience it as bodily extension, not UI interaction. Breaking a deeply incorporated interaction pattern (the Hamburger menu where the back swipe used to be) feels like a body violation, not an inconvenience. This is not hyperbole — phenomenologically, it is a disruption of the body schema.

For mobile tools: phone interaction is already deeply incorporated for most users. Design within that existing body schema. Gestural interactions that feel natural are those that map to it, not novel gestures that require learning new bodily vocabulary at the moment of use.

## Maslow — Peak Experiences & Transcendence

Abraham Maslow's later work (after the hierarchy) focused on peak experiences — moments of intense joy, awe, or insight that feel complete in themselves and are remembered as among the most important of a person's life.

**Characteristics of peak experiences:**
- Perception becomes richly multisensory and present-focused
- The boundary between self and world becomes permeable
- Time perception alters (usually slows or disappears)
- The experience is intrinsically meaningful — not pointing toward anything else
- They are recalled as high points regardless of duration

**Conditions that facilitate peak experiences:**
- Reduced self-consciousness (the critic goes quiet)
- Full present-moment absorption
- Beauty or excellence encountered at an intensity that exceeds ordinary response
- Feeling of belonging or connection that transcends the ordinary social surface

For tools used within experience-rich environments: the tool's job is to *not interrupt* peak moments and to *support the conditions* for them — reduced decision load, minimal self-consciousness, present-moment cues. The tool that produces memories of *using the tool* has failed its actual purpose.

## Transpersonal Psychology (Grof)

Stanislav Grof's work (initially from LSD research in psychiatry, later from holotropic breathwork) documented states of consciousness that exceed the ordinary biographical self: reliving birth and perinatal experience, encounters with collective cultural material, experiences of unity, dissolution of self-other boundary, and access to non-personal states of being.

Grof classified these as **transpersonal experiences** — beyond the person as ordinarily defined. Whether the metaphysics are taken literally or treated as phenomenological description, the structural account is useful:

**The spectrum of consciousness:**
Ordinary waking consciousness → altered states (dream, trance, ecstatic) → transpersonal states (dissolution of ordinary subject/object division)

Festivals at their best push participants along this spectrum — away from ordinary waking consciousness toward something more porous and collective. This is why festivals work on people in a way that watching the same performances on video does not: the collective physical experience creates conditions for states not accessible in ordinary individual consciousness.

**For design:** the app cannot produce these states. It can avoid destroying them. Design for minimum disruption of altered and elevated states. No jarring alerts, no complex decisions, no reminders that pull the user back into ordinary biographical self-management. When the user is in a flow or peak state, the app should be invisible.

## Turner & van Gennep — Ritual Theory & Liminality

Victor Turner (building on Arnold van Gennep's *Rites of Passage*, 1909) gave rigorous anthropological structure to why threshold experiences transform people.

**Van Gennep's three-phase structure:**
1. **Separation** — the individual is detached from their ordinary social position and identity
2. **Liminality** — the threshold state: betwixt and between. Ordinary rules are suspended. Identity is fluid. The person is neither what they were nor yet what they will be.
3. **Reincorporation** — the individual re-enters society with a new status

**Turner's additions:**

**Liminality:** the liminal phase is characterized by disorientation, ambiguity, and potency. It is dangerous and creative simultaneously — precisely because the ordinary social structure is suspended, new possibilities become visible. Liminal figures (pilgrims, initiates, festival-goers) wear unusual costumes, submit to unusual conditions, and experience unusual solidarity.

**Communitas:** the form of social bond that emerges in liminal states. Not the structured, role-differentiated bonds of ordinary social life, but spontaneous, undifferentiated, egalitarian solidarity. The festival crowd at peak — everyone moving together, individual boundaries dissolved — is communitas. This is why people describe festivals as "the only place where I feel like everyone is on the same side."

**For experience-design contexts:**
- Threshold events (concerts, ceremonies, retreats, gatherings) are designed liminal experiences. The entrance is a separation rite; the space is the threshold; the exit is reincorporation.
- Design should honor this liminal quality. Features that reassert ordinary life (work notifications, social pressure) are anti-liminal. Features that support the threshold quality (mark the moment, orient without pulling back to ordinary self-management) are liminal-aligned.
- Aesthetic choices can themselves be liminal costumes — alternative identities available only within the experience.

## The Sublime (Kant, Burke, Lyotard)

The sublime is an aesthetic experience that exceeds ordinary perception — that overwhelms, expands, and temporarily dissolves the ordinary sense of self as bounded and in control.

**Burke's physical sublime** (*A Philosophical Enquiry*, 1757): the sublime arises from vastness, obscurity, darkness, power, infinity — things that produce astonishment (a kind of pleasurable terror). The sublime is physically overwhelming. Loud music at dangerous volumes. Vastness of sky or crowd. The experience that takes the breath away.

**Kant's cognitive sublime** (*Critique of Judgment*, 1790): the sublime occurs when experience exceeds our capacity to comprehend it as a bounded object. Reason reaches its limit and something else — a sense of the self's own depth and dignity — fills the gap. The feeling of being overwhelmed by magnitude or power, yet surviving it, produces the sublime response.

**Lyotard's postmodern sublime** (*The Postmodern Condition*, 1979): the sublime as the presentation of the unpresentable — what cannot be contained in any form. The appropriate aesthetic for an era where grand narratives have collapsed is the attempt to present what cannot be fully presented.

**For experience design:** the sublime is often not a side effect — it is the goal. Design's role when targeting the sublime: don't prevent it, don't interrupt it, and — if possible — amplify the conditions that approach it.

The technological sublime borrows from this register: the overwhelming complexity of data made visible, the sense of more being present than can be processed, the aesthetic of systems at the edge of legibility.

## Quantum Consciousness (speculative, contested)

*Labeled explicitly as contested and speculative. Included for the discovery phase — to be used as provocation and exploration, not as established framework.*

Several thinkers have proposed that consciousness has a quantum-mechanical basis that distinguishes it from classical computation and creates genuine indeterminacy:

**Penrose-Hameroff Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR):** Penrose (*The Emperor's New Mind*, 1989; *Shadows of the Mind*, 1994) argued that human mathematical insight exceeds what any classical algorithm can produce, implying a non-computational basis for consciousness. Hameroff proposed microtubules in neurons as the quantum substrate. The theory remains contested and lacks empirical confirmation.

**Bohm's implicate order** (*Wholeness and the Implicate Order*, 1980): physicist David Bohm proposed that ordinary physical reality (the explicate order) unfolds from a deeper implicate order — a holographic, undivided field in which all things are enfolded. Consciousness participates in this implicate order rather than observing it from outside. Non-local correlations, synchronicities, and collective states might reflect the implicate structure surfacing.

**Consciousness causes collapse (Copenhagen-derived):** In some interpretations of quantum mechanics, the act of observation (which requires a conscious observer) causes the wave function to collapse from superposition to a definite state. This makes consciousness causally relevant to physical reality — a position most physicists reject but that has generated substantial philosophical interest.

**What it gestures at for design:**
If consciousness is non-local, non-linear, and participatory rather than merely computational — the kind of collective states that festivals produce (communitas, shared peak experience, synchrony) may be more than social coordination. They may involve genuine non-local resonance between conscious systems. This is speculative. It is worth holding as a possibility while designing for experiences that go beyond the individual.

The practical implication (even for skeptics): design for collective resonance, not just individual user journeys. What is the experience when many users are in the same space or state simultaneously? Does the design account for that layer?
