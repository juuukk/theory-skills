# Values & Ethics: Dieter Rams + Papanek + Emotional Design

## Dieter Rams' 10 Principles of Good Design

### Why it matters
Rams developed these principles over decades at Braun and Vitsœ, articulating what separates design that serves from design that performs. They remain the clearest statement of design as a discipline of restraint and honesty — and they implicitly contain a critique of most consumer design: the disposable, the fashionable, the dishonest, the excessive.

### The 10 principles in depth

**1. Good design is innovative**
Innovation is not novelty. Innovation means using the possibilities of materials, production, and technology to solve problems in genuinely better ways. Rams' innovation was in service of function — not differentiation for its own sake. Ask: what does this technology genuinely enable that wasn't possible before?

**2. Good design makes a product useful**
A product is bought to be used. Usability is primary; everything else serves it or gets cut. Design that looks good but works poorly has failed the most basic requirement.

**3. Good design is aesthetic**
Aesthetic quality is a component of function, not a layer on top of it. Well-designed objects that are used daily affect well-being — their visual quality is part of their utility. But Rams' aesthetic is specific: clarity, order, and purposeful form — not decoration.

**4. Good design makes a product understandable**
Good design clarifies. At best, it makes a product self-explanatory. The product communicates its function, its structure, and how to use it through its form — without instructions, without help text, without training.

**5. Good design is unobtrusive**
Products are tools, not art objects or status signals. A well-designed tool fulfills its purpose and gets out of the way. It doesn't demand attention; it enables the user's goal. Design that calls attention to itself above its function has missed the point.

**6. Good design is honest**
It doesn't make a product appear more powerful, innovative, or valuable than it actually is. It doesn't manipulate or promise what it can't deliver. Honest design builds trust; dishonest design earns short-term attention and long-term erosion of credibility.

**7. Good design is long-lasting**
It avoids being fashionable — and therefore avoids becoming obsolete when fashion changes. It has a quality of timelessness that comes from solving the underlying problem well, not from following the aesthetic of a moment. Planned obsolescence is a design failure.

**8. Good design is thorough down to the last detail**
Nothing is arbitrary. Nothing is left to chance. Care at the detail level communicates respect for the user. The quality of the whole is determined by the quality of its weakest moment — the interaction a user has that reveals carelessness or shortcuts.

**9. Good design is environmentally friendly**
Design conserves resources and minimizes waste across the entire lifecycle — manufacturing, use, and end of life. Rams saw environmental responsibility as inseparable from good design long before sustainability was mainstream. *Extension:* emerging digital sustainability frameworks (Web Sustainability Guidelines) apply equivalent thinking to digital products: page weight, energy-efficient hosting, unnecessary data transfer as design failures.

**10. Good design is as little design as possible**
Less but better. Concentrate on the essential. Don't burden a product with non-essentials. Purity of form results from purity of purpose. This is the hardest principle: it requires knowing what to leave out, which requires understanding the essence.

### Audit questions
- Does every design element serve a function, or does some of it exist for decoration or differentiation?
- Is the design honest about what the product can and cannot do?
- Will this design still feel appropriate in 10 years, or is it following a current fashion?
- Are there elements that could be removed without loss?
- Does the product communicate its own use without requiring instructions?

## Victor Papanek: Design for the Real World

### Why it matters
Papanek's 1971 critique of the design profession remains the most direct peer-backed argument that design has ethical obligations it routinely ignores. His thesis: most professional design serves the interests of manufacturers and advertisers — not users, and certainly not the majority of humanity. The design profession's job, as it has historically been practiced, is to make things people don't need that are built to fail so they have to be replaced.

This isn't nostalgia — it's a lens that becomes more important as design decisions affect more people at scale through digital products.

### Core principles

**Design for need, not manufactured want**
The distinction: needs are real (safe shelter, clear information, reliable tools). Wants can be manufactured by advertising and social pressure (the next version, the premium tier, the status signal). Most commercial design serves manufactured want. Papanek argued designers should ask: does this solve a real problem, or is it creating a problem to sell a solution?

**Design for the majority world**
Most of the world's design is created by a small number of people (predominantly wealthy, Western, male) for a relatively small global market (predominantly wealthy, Western). The majority of humanity — who are poorer, less urban, often disabled, often elderly, often non-English-speaking — is treated as a secondary market if at all. Design that ignores this is not neutral; it actively excludes.

**Design without harm**
Every design decision has consequences that extend beyond the immediate user: to workers who make it, to communities affected by its production, to environments that absorb its waste, to future people who inherit its effects. Papanek insisted designers are morally responsible for these consequences. "There are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few."

**Planned obsolescence as design failure**
Designing products to fail — or to become unfashionable — so that users replace them is a fundamental ethical failure. It wastes resources, creates false cost for users, and serves shareholder return over human welfare. In digital: features deprecated to drive upgrades, data formats that prevent migration, and artificial incompatibilities are the digital equivalent.

### Design Justice lens (Costanza-Chock, 2020)
Extends Papanek's ethical framework with intersectional analysis:
- **Who was in the room when this was made?** The demographics of design teams shape what problems get solved and for whom. Homogeneous teams have systematic blind spots.
- **Who benefits from this design?** Cui bono — follow the value extraction.
- **Who bears the costs?** Often those least represented in the design process.
- **Whose knowledge counts?** User research that treats affected communities as subjects rather than collaborators is a form of extraction.
- **What would it look like if the design process centered those most impacted?**

### Audit questions
- Does this design solve a real need or a manufactured want?
- Who is excluded from this design, and why?
- What harms might this design cause to users, workers, communities, or environments?
- Is longevity designed in, or is obsolescence?
- Who was in the room when this was designed?
- If the people most affected by this design had led it, what would be different?

## Emotional Design (Norman)

### Why it matters
Norman's emotional design framework, from his 2004 book of the same name, explains a common design mystery: why do users prefer a product that is objectively less functional, or tolerate frustration from a product they love? Emotion is not separate from usability — it is intertwined with it. Understanding the three levels explains why technically correct design can still feel wrong.

### The three levels

**Visceral**
The immediate, automatic, pre-conscious response to sensory qualities: color, shape, texture, sound, smell, movement. This response happens before cognition — before the user has read a word or understood a function. Beautiful things evoke positive affect; jarring things create resistance.

Visceral design is not superficial. The initial emotional state it creates colors everything that follows (see: Aesthetic-Usability Effect). A beautiful interface gets the benefit of the doubt when it's frustrating; an ugly one is blamed for failures it didn't cause.

Design levers: visual craft, motion quality, sound design, material quality, proportion, color.

**Behavioral**
The experience of using the thing. Does it work? Does it feel good to operate? Is the feedback satisfying? This is the domain of classical usability — but Norman's addition is the affective dimension: the pleasure of a well-placed interaction, the frustration of friction, the satisfaction of competence.

Behavioral design success: the interface disappears. The user is doing their task, not managing the interface. Failure: the interface imposes itself — forces the user to think about the tool rather than the goal.

Design levers: interaction design, feedback quality, timing, efficiency, error handling.

**Reflective**
The highest, most conscious level. This is the story the user tells about themselves in relation to the product: what does using this say about me? Does this reflect the kind of person I am or want to be? This is the level of brand, culture, meaning, and self-image.

A Leica camera is not necessarily the best tool for capturing images. But it carries a story about craft, seriousness, and vision that its owners want to participate in. A luxury product is not just functional; it is a statement. This is not vanity — it's a legitimate human need to find meaning in the objects we use.

Reflective design failures: a product that works well but feels embarrassing to use; a service that is functional but makes the user feel surveilled or disrespected; a brand that communicates values the user doesn't endorse.

Design levers: brand narrative, language and tone, cultural positioning, values alignment, community.

### Diagnosing emotional design problems
When something "feels off" despite being technically correct, run through the three levels:
- Is it viscerally jarring? (color, form, motion, material quality)
- Is it behaviorally frustrating? (friction, poor feedback, inefficiency)
- Is it reflectively misaligned? (brand mismatch, wrong tone, wrong story)

Often the diagnosis is at a different level than the proposed fix.

### Audit questions
- What is the immediate visceral response to this design? Is it appropriate for the context and audience?
- Is the interaction experience smooth enough that the interface disappears?
- What story does using this product tell about the user? Is that a story they want to be part of?
- Are there emotional design failures at one level being compensated for (poorly) at another?

## Critical Theory & Political Economy of Design

### Why it matters
Papanek and Costanza-Chock provide the ethical framework. Critical theory provides the structural analysis of why design so reliably fails that framework — the systemic forces that make exploitative design the default rather than the exception.

### Commodity fetishism (Marx)
In capitalist production, social relations between people (designer, worker, user, owner) become concealed behind relations between things. A product appears as a self-contained object with inherent value rather than as a crystallization of human labor under specific social conditions.

Applied to design: "user experience" is separated in practice from the conditions of the product's making and the interests it serves. Good UX is presented as serving users; often it serves retention metrics, ad revenue, or lock-in. The fetish conceals the actual relationships.

**Audit question:** who actually benefits from this design, and how does the design conceal that?

### Alienation in design systems
Marx's alienation (Entfremdung) — the worker's separation from the product of labor, from other workers, from species-being — has a direct design-system analogue: design that separates users from understanding the systems they use produces dependence and disempowerment. Proprietary data formats, platform lock-in, designed-in obsolescence, and dark patterns all produce this at scale.

Counter-design: open systems, interoperability, user data ownership, legible design that shows its seams.

### Political economy of attention
The attention economy treats human attention as the scarce resource platforms compete for. The political economy analysis extends this: attention extraction is not symmetric. Platforms extract attention from users and convert it to ad revenue without users receiving commensurate value. This is structural, not accidental.

**The audit lens:** every engagement-driving pattern should be evaluated for whether it creates value for the user or extracts value from the user. The patterns can look identical; the economic relationship is not.

### Spectacle (Debord)
Guy Debord's *Society of the Spectacle* (1967): modern society replaces authentic lived experience with its representation. "All that was once directly lived has become mere representation."

**For any experience-adjacent design:** the risk is real. Design that turns presence into content (capture the moment for social media, earn points by documenting rather than doing) converts authentic experience into spectacle. Resist this even when engagement metrics reward it.

### Audit questions (critical theory)
- Who benefits from this design at the structural level? Who bears the costs?
- Does this design conceal the interests it serves?
- Is it extracting attention/value from users in exchange for less value than it takes?
- Does it convert authentic experience into spectacle?
- What would this design look like if it maximized user sovereignty rather than platform metrics?
